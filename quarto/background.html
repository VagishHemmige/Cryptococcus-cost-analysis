Clinical background

Author

Vagish Hemmige

This page summarizes what is known about cryptococcosis in kidney transplant recipients and about its cost, and the gap this study addresses. How the study answers its question is set out under Study design and methods.

Cryptococcosis after kidney transplant

Cryptococcosis is the third most common invasive fungal infection in solid organ transplant recipients1. In a multicenter US surveillance network, it accounted for 8% of invasive fungal infections in organ transplant recipients, and kidney recipients had the lowest one-year incidence of any invasive fungal infection of all organ types2. In inpatient data from three US states, 0.32% of kidney recipients had cryptococcosis coded during follow-up3. Earlier, in USRDS data from 1994 to 1997, cryptococcosis was among the leading causes of hospitalized fungal infection after kidney transplant4.

Most infections occur well after transplant. Median onset was 616 days after kidney transplant in the three-state cohort3, and 575 days for cryptococcosis across organ types in the surveillance network2. Among kidney recipients hospitalized for cryptococcosis in a national USRDS cohort, the median time from the most recent transplant to the index hospitalization was 30.5 months5.

Transmission through the donated organ is rare. From 2009 to 2019, US reports to the transplant network’s disease-transmission committee identified 12 donors with proven or probable transmission, to 23 recipients; for another 11 donors, recipients received prophylaxis and transmission was averted6. Donor-derived infection usually presents early after transplant6, so it is context for this study rather than its focus.

Presentation, treatment and outcomes

Onset can be insidious1, and the central nervous system is often involved: in a national USRDS cohort of 1,265 kidney recipients hospitalized for cryptococcosis from 2006 to 2016, 51% had meningitis5. Guidelines recommend lumbar puncture for suspected or proven disease, and treatment of meningitis or disseminated disease with induction therapy, a lipid formulation of amphotericin B with flucytosine, followed by consolidation with fluconazole1; a 2024 global guideline updates these recommendations across patient groups7. US guidelines recommend at least 2 weeks of induction and at least 8 weeks of consolidation8, so treatment lasts at least 10 weeks. Lipid formulations of amphotericin B were associated with lower 90-day mortality than the deoxycholate formulation in transplant recipients with central nervous system disease9. Reducing immunosuppression during treatment can lead to immune reconstitution syndrome1, which has been linked to graft loss in kidney recipients10.

Outcomes are poor. In the national USRDS cohort, 22% of kidney recipients hospitalized for cryptococcosis died within six months, 44% required intensive care and 49% had acute kidney injury5. In a prospective multicenter cohort of 111 organ transplant recipients, 61% had disseminated disease and 14% died within 90 days; mortality was lower in those receiving a calcineurin inhibitor11. In a French cohort of 88 kidney recipients diagnosed from 2002 to 2020, 33% died within 12 months, and about a third of recipients lost their graft by last follow-up, with or without meningitis12.

What is known about its cost

Published US estimates are of hospital stays, of modelled treatment, or of costs over time without a comparison group:

  • Per hospitalization. National estimates put cryptococcosis at 4,755 hospitalizations in 2014, with mean payments per stay of about $39,000 to $70,000 depending on the payer (in 2017 dollars)13. In inpatient data from 18 states for 1997 to 2009, median costs per hospitalization for cryptococcal meningitis were about $15,700 to $16,800, depending on HIV status14.
  • Excess cost compared with similar patients. Two studies compared patients with and without fungal infection, with all fungal species together: about $55,400 in excess cost per hospital stay in transplant recipients with invasive fungal infection, in national inpatient data for 2004 and 200515; and an average attributable cost of $31,200 per patient for systemic fungal infections, including cryptococcosis, from inpatient costs and modelled outpatient costs for 199816.
  • Treatment. A cost-effectiveness model estimated costs of care for cryptococcal meningitis of about $45,000 to $83,000 depending on the induction regimen, at 2015 US prices, when flucytosine cost about $2,000 a day in the United States8.
  • Over time. In commercial claims, patients with cryptococcal meningitis accrued mean charges of $103,236 over up to five years after diagnosis, with no comparison group and in charges rather than payments17.

Excess Medicare cost after other infections in kidney transplant recipients has been estimated in USRDS data. Pneumonia or sepsis in the first year after transplant added about $40,000 to $50,000 in first-year Medicare payments18. We found no study that estimates the excess Medicare payments attributable to cryptococcosis over the months after diagnosis in kidney transplant recipients, compared with similar recipients at the same time since transplant.

Why this question matters

An estimate of the cost attributable to cryptococcosis over the months after diagnosis, rather than of one hospital stay, is what payers and transplant programs need to judge its burden, and what economic models need when weighing prevention, such as donor screening or prophylaxis, against treatment. Costs may also begin before the recorded diagnosis: in state inpatient data, a third of HIV-negative patients hospitalized with cryptococcosis had a hospital admission suggestive of the disease in the preceding 90 days19. The study’s monthly cost profile begins three months before diagnosis.

This study asks: among adult kidney transplant recipients, how much more does Medicare pay in the 12 months after cryptococcosis is diagnosed than for similar recipients at the same time since transplant who have not been diagnosed? Study design and methods explains how it is answered.
