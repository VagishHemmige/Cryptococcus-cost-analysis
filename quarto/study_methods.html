Study design and methods

Author

Vagish Hemmige

This page explains, in plain language and without code, what this study chose and why. The general background it relies on, such as what USRDS and Medicare claims are and how risk-set matching works, is on Methodological background. The formal specification is the analysis plan; each section below ends by linking the part of the plan that governs it. Three kinds of note mark where the description stands:

  • Specified in: the section of the analysis plan that defines the method.
  • Current code: where the code does not yet do what the plan says, what it does now, with a link to the GitHub issue tracking the difference.
  • Decision pending: a choice the plan has not yet made. These are listed in the plan’s open decisions.

This is a methods-and-code site. No results are shown, because the analysis is not complete and the data cannot be shared.

The question and the design in brief

Question. Among adult kidney transplant recipients, how much more does Medicare pay for a patient’s care in the 12 months after cryptococcosis is diagnosed than it would have paid had they not developed it?

Approach. Each patient diagnosed with cryptococcosis (a case) is compared with up to two similar recipients who have reached the same time since transplant without being diagnosed (controls). Cases and controls are followed for 12 months from the same starting point, and the difference in their Medicare payments is the excess cost of cryptococcosis.

What is estimated. The average excess cost among people who develop cryptococcosis (the average treatment effect in the treated, or ATT): the mean difference in total Medicare payments over months 0 to 11 between cases and their matched controls, per case.

Specified in: §1 Objective, §2 Design, §8.3 Estimand.

Data used

The data come from the United States Renal Data System and its linked Medicare claims, described on Methodological background. This study uses the patient, transplant, payer history, treatment history and death files, and Medicare Part A and Part B claims. Part D prescription records are planned only where a covariate needs them. Claims from 2006 to 2021 are used, cases are diagnosed between 2007 and 2020, and follow-up ends on 1 January 2022. The data are used under a data use agreement and are not published.

Both institutional and physician/supplier claims are used (Medicare claims). Because claims are complete only under fee-for-service primary coverage (Medicare as primary payer), a person is observed only during periods when fee-for-service Medicare is their primary payer for both Part A and Part B, as recorded in the USRDS payer history file. Many kidney transplant recipients lose Medicare coverage over time: Medicare entitlement based only on kidney failure ends 36 months after a successful transplant, unless the person qualifies another way (for example by age or disability). The study’s findings therefore apply to recipients with fee-for-service Medicare as their primary payer.

Specified in: §3 Data source, §4.2 Eligibility, §11 Limitations.

Current code: two payer history categories count as coverage: “Medicare fee-for-service primary pay, for both Part A and Part B” and “Medicare fee-for-service primary pay, for other”. This follows the USRDS Researcher’s Guide definition of Medicare as primary payer. The guide does not say which Medicare parts the second category covers.

Decision pending: whether other payer arrangements, such as Medicare as secondary payer, are ever eligible (D1).

Who is studied

The source population is kidney transplant recipients in USRDS with a transplant before 2021. Cases and controls must meet the same criteria at their own time zero (defined below). A person is eligible if they:

  1. have had at least one kidney transplant before that date;
  2. are aged 18 or older;
  3. have no earlier cryptococcosis diagnosis;
  4. have had fee-for-service Medicare as primary payer for Parts A and B for the whole 365 days before that date, without gaps, so that their baseline health and baseline cost are fully observed;
  5. are alive and under observation on that date.

The time scale is time since the most recent kidney transplant. People with more than one transplant are eligible, and their earlier transplants are counted as a covariate.

Specified in: §4 Study population.

Current code:

  • The 365-day coverage window is counted back from time zero, and it can begin before the transplant, for example in a recipient covered by Medicare during dialysis. The plan does not say whether the window must fall after transplant.
  • Recipients of a kidney–pancreas transplant are included. A pancreas transplant flag taken from diagnosis codes is described in the baseline table, but the transplant records are not used to identify these recipients, and the flag is not used in matching or the models.
  • A diagnosis on the day of the first transplant is treated as a diagnosis before transplant, and the patient is excluded.

Decision pending: whether kidney–pancreas and other multi-organ recipients are excluded or included with an indicator (D2).

Identifying cryptococcosis and time zero

How a case is found in claims

Cryptococcosis is identified from diagnosis codes on Medicare claims: ICD-9-CM codes for services before 1 October 2015 and ICD-10-CM codes from that date on. The code list is shown on the Setup page.

The current rule counts a diagnosis as established on the first of:

  • one inpatient claim carrying a cryptococcosis code; or
  • a second record of any other kind carrying a code: hospital outpatient, physician/supplier, skilled nursing, home health or other institutional claims.

A code in any diagnosis position counts. There is no time limit between the two records, and two on the same day count as two. Records are counted one at a time rather than grouped into visits, so a single outpatient visit that lists the code more than once may be enough. The diagnosis date is the first day of service on the record that establishes the diagnosis.

Specified in: §5.1 Exposure definition (the code list), §5.2 Diagnosis date. The plan gives the diagnosis date as the service date of the “first qualifying claim”. Under a two-claim rule that could mean the first coded claim or the one that completes the rule; the code uses the second.

Decision pending: the claim algorithm, meaning which claim types and diagnosis positions count, how many claims are needed and within what window (D3). The rule above is what the code does now.

What a claims-based definition can and cannot show

A claim shows that a clinician recorded the diagnosis for billing. It does not show that the infection was confirmed by culture, antigen testing or histopathology, and codes are sometimes recorded while a diagnosis is being ruled out. Some cases will therefore be misclassified (Finding a diagnosis in claims).

The 365-day window of complete coverage before diagnosis means that, had the rule been met earlier in that year, it would have been seen. An earlier single record carrying the code can still fall inside the window: when the diagnosis is established by a second record, the first always comes before it. Complete coverage makes it more likely the diagnosis is new, but it does not prove the infection began then. A person with a diagnosis before their first transplant is excluded.

Two planned sensitivity analyses address this: a stricter claims definition, and separate analyses of diagnoses before and after the October 2015 change from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM (§9).

Time zero

Time zero is the day from which costs are counted, and before which everything used for matching or adjustment is measured.

  • Cases: the diagnosis date.
  • Controls: the day on which the control reaches the same number of days since their own most recent transplant as the case had at diagnosis.

For example, if a case is diagnosed 243 days after their transplant, each of their controls has time zero 243 days after the control’s own transplant. That is usually a different calendar date from the case’s. The study timeline on the home page shows how time zero, the eligibility lookback and the monthly cost windows fit together.

Specified in: §5.3 Time zero.

Choosing comparison patients

Risk-set matching

Controls are chosen by risk-set matching (Risk-set matching). For each case, the risk set is everyone who, at the case’s time since transplant, is still eligible and has not been diagnosed.

Two reasons make time since transplant the right time scale:

  • Both the risk of infection and the cost of care change with time since transplant, so cases and controls are compared at the same point in their post-transplant course.
  • Choosing controls among everyone ever transplanted without cryptococcosis would compare cases with people whose follow-up was selected using the future. Risk-set matching uses only what was known at the case’s time zero.

Under the plan, a person who is diagnosed later can serve as a control before their own diagnosis.

Specified in: §5.4 Control eligibility, §6 Matching.

Current code: only people who are never diagnosed are used as controls (#7).

Decision pending: whether one person may serve as a control in more than one risk set, as in standard incidence density sampling, or at most once (D4).

What cases and controls are matched on

Under the plan, whichever matching method is chosen:

  • cases and controls have the same current graft status (functioning, or failed and on dialysis);
  • each case has up to 2 controls;
  • cases are matched in a random order fixed by a recorded seed, and ties between controls are broken at random;
  • cost in the 3 months before time zero is not used for matching. It is kept to check whether cases and controls had similar costs before diagnosis.

The plan sets out two matching methods, one of each kind described under Ways of matching, and has not chosen between them:

  • Option A, direct covariate matching: exact matching on diabetes, cirrhosis and HIV, limits on the difference in age and in calendar date, and preference for the control closest in earlier cost (months −12 to −4). Covariates not matched on are checked for balance and, if imbalanced, adjusted for in the outcome model.
  • Option B, time-varying propensity score matching: a model estimates each person’s probability of being diagnosed in each month from all the covariates in the plan, and each case is matched to the controls with the closest score at the same time since transplant, within 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the score.

The covariates cover demographics, transplant history, graft function, immunosuppression intensity, comorbidity, other infections, earlier use of care, earlier cost, dual Medicaid eligibility and region, all measured before time zero.

Specified in: §6.1 Common elements, §6.2 Covariates, §6.3 Option A, §6.4 Option B.

Current code: the code implements a form of Option A. Controls are matched exactly on cirrhosis, HIV and diabetes, within 10 years of age and with time zero within 3 years of the case’s, and a control with the same number of transplants is preferred. Each control is used once. It differs from the plan in these ways:

  • cases are matched in order of how few potential controls they have, not in random order;
  • among eligible controls, the first in row order is chosen, so the recorded seed has no effect (#3);
  • graft status and earlier cost are not matched on (#8);
  • the count of potential controls used to order cases checks Medicare coverage on a different date from the matching itself (#6);
  • cases for whom no control is found remain in the matched data (#12).

Decision pending: the lookback window for rejection treatment and the comorbidity index (D5); the matching method (D10); and, under Option B, whether to keep the limits on age and calendar date as well (D6).

Checking the match

A flow diagram follows the cohort from the source population through eligibility, case identification and matching, and baseline characteristics are reported by group before and after matching. Balance between cases and controls is judged with standardized mean differences at time zero, for every covariate in the plan and for cost in each of months −3, −2 and −1. An absolute standardized mean difference below 0.1 is taken as adequate. The number of cases with 2, 1 and 0 controls is reported, and unmatched cases are compared with matched ones.

Specified in: §6.5 Matching outcomes reported, §8.1 Descriptive analysis, §8.2 Balance.

Turning claims into costs

Payments, not charges

Cost is measured as the amount Medicare paid; charges are not used (Payments, not charges). Payments made by patients, other insurers and Medicare Part D are not included.

Two payment fields are used: the Medicare payment on each institutional claim, and the Medicare payment on each physician/supplier line. Outpatient, home health and hospice claims also list payments for each revenue center (each department or type of service on the bill). These describe the same services as the claim’s payment, so the two are never added together.

Specified in: §7.1 Cost measure; exclusions in §11 Limitations. The payment fields and the rule against adding revenue-center payments to claim payments come from the repository’s AGENTS.md.

Current code: a separate 365-day summary also totals revenue-center payments, but separately from the claim totals, not added to them. The monthly costs used in the models come from institutional claim payments and physician/supplier line payments only.

Spreading a stay across days

An institutional claim that covers several days is spread evenly across them (Spreading a stay across days has a worked example).

Specified in: §7.1 Cost measure.

Current code: institutional claims are spread across days in the monthly costs. Physician/supplier lines are assigned to their first day of service. In the separate 365-day summary, institutional costs are spread across days but then selected by the claim’s first day (#11).

Adjusting for inflation

All costs are expressed in January 2021 US dollars.

Specified in: §7.2 Inflation adjustment.

Current code: costs are adjusted with the medical care component of the Consumer Price Index, according to the month in which each claim began.

Decision pending: the price index (D7).

Months, partial months, and missing versus zero

Each person’s follow-up is divided into 30-day months counted from time zero: month 0 is days 0 to 29, month −3 is days −90 to −61, and month 11, the last, is days 330 to 359. A person-month’s cost is the sum of the spread, inflation-adjusted payments for that month. Covered months with no claims count as zero, uncovered months are left out, and partly observed months are kept with their days observed (Missing versus zero cost).

Specified in: §7.3 Person-month structure.

Current code: a monthly total below zero, which can arise from claim adjustments, is set to zero. The plan does not yet describe this step. The separate 365-day summary covers days 0 to 365 (366 days), while the monthly costs cover days 0 to 359 (#13).

End of follow-up

Follow-up ends at the earliest of:

  • day 365 after time zero;
  • death;
  • loss of fee-for-service Medicare as primary payer;
  • 1 January 2022.

Graft failure after time zero does not end follow-up. It is one of the ways cryptococcosis may lead to higher cost.

How death is handled defines what is being estimated (Death and what is estimated). If death ends follow-up, the estimate describes the excess cost of care while people are alive. If the months after death count as zero cost, the estimate also reflects the cost consequences of mortality. Whichever is chosen as primary, the other is a sensitivity analysis.

Specified in: §7.4 End of follow-up.

Current code: follow-up ends at day 365, at 1 January 2022, or at the end of the period of Medicare coverage containing time zero. Death is not yet an end point (#10).

Decision pending: the treatment of death (D8).

Statistical analysis

The primary model

Why the model describes mean cost directly is explained under Why cost data are hard to model. Each person-month’s cost is modelled with a generalized linear model that has:

  • a log link and a Tweedie distribution;
  • terms for group (case or control), month (as a category) and their interaction, so that the difference between cases and controls can differ in every month;
  • the logarithm of the number of days observed as an offset.

What each of these does is explained under Models for costs. Standard errors come from generalized estimating equations clustered on matched set, and also on patient if one person can appear in more than one matched set.

Specified in: §8.4 Primary model.

From the model to a dollar estimate

  1. For each month, the model predicts the mean cost of a full 30-day month for cases and for controls.
  2. The case-minus-control difference is taken for each month.
  3. The differences for months 0 to 11 are added up. That total is the excess cost per case.
  4. A 95% confidence interval comes from a cluster bootstrap: matched sets are resampled with replacement 1,000 times (or more if needed), and the whole calculation is repeated each time.

The same model gives the month-by-month profile of excess cost from month −3 to month 11, and fitting it separately to each care setting (inpatient, outpatient, skilled nursing, home health, hospice, dialysis, physician/supplier) divides the excess by setting.

Specified in: §8.4 Primary model, §8.5 Secondary analyses.

Decision pending: whether the estimate relies on matching alone, with months −3 to −1 reported as a balance check, or subtracts the difference between cases and controls before diagnosis as well, which gives a difference-in-differences estimate (D9).

What the code does now

The code fits five models to each of ten monthly cost outcomes: total cost, institutional cost, physician/supplier cost, and seven institutional categories (inpatient, outpatient, skilled nursing, home health, hospice, dialysis, and a non-claim/auxiliary category). None is yet the primary model as specified, and the total currently reported is not yet the planned estimate.

Model Outcome scale Correlation handled by Role in the plan
Tweedie, log link, mixed model Dollars Random intercept per patient Closest to the primary model, but the plan uses generalized estimating equations clustered on matched set
Gaussian, identity link, mixed model Dollars Random intercept per patient Sensitivity analysis
Gaussian, identity link, generalized estimating equations Dollars Exchangeable correlation within patient Sensitivity analysis; currently used for the reported total
Gaussian on log(cost + 1), mixed model Log dollars Random intercept per patient Sensitivity analysis
Gaussian on log(cost + 1), generalized estimating equations Log dollars Exchangeable correlation within patient Sensitivity analysis

Known differences from the plan:

  • the reported total adds up each month’s case–control difference measured relative to month −3, including months −2 and −1, so it is not the sum of differences over months 0 to 11 (#2);
  • the models on the dollar and log(cost + 1) scales include the days-observed offset in a way that only makes sense with a log link (#4);
  • the calculation for log-link models adds ratios rather than dollar differences (#5);
  • plots label back-transformed predictions from the log(cost + 1) and mixed models as mean dollars (#14);
  • the identifier of each matched set is not carried into the modelling data, so models can cluster only on patient (see also #12);
  • the cluster bootstrap is not yet implemented.

Missing covariate data

Cost is observed only during covered months, by design. For covariates from the registry, missingness is described, and if it exceeds 5% for any covariate in a propensity score, multiple imputation by chained equations is used.

Specified in: §8.6 Missing data.

Sensitivity analyses

The planned sensitivity analyses test whether the result depends on particular choices:

  • a sequential target-trial emulation, which compares people newly diagnosed in each month with everyone else eligible that month. The comparison group is weighted by the odds of diagnosis from a propensity score rather than matched, with weights truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles and untruncated results also reported;
  • the other way of handling death (D8);
  • outcome models on the dollar scale and on log cost;
  • a stricter claims definition of cryptococcosis;
  • one control per case, exact matching on cytomegalovirus infection, a narrower propensity score caliper under Option B, and the matching method not chosen as primary;
  • recipients of a first transplant only;
  • diagnoses before and after the October 2015 change in diagnosis codes.

Specified in: §9 Sensitivity analyses, Appendix A.

Disclosure rules

No table, figure, diagram or text shows a count under 11, including counts that could be worked out from other cells. Figures that plot individual patients are not published. Every output is checked against the data use agreement before release.

Specified in: §10 Disclosure control.

Reporting and limitations

The study will be reported following STROBE, its RECORD extension for routinely collected health data, and, for the target-trial analysis, TARGET. The limitations to be addressed include misclassification of diagnoses in claims, the restriction to Medicare payments and to fee-for-service Medicare, confounding by immunosuppression intensity that claims cannot fully measure, and coverage rules that change over time.

Specified in: §11 Limitations, §12 Reporting map.

- cases are matched in a random order fixed by a recorded seed, and ties between controls are broken at random;
- cost in the 3 months before time zero is **not** used for matching. It is kept to check whether cases and controls had similar costs before diagnosis.

The plan sets out two matching methods, one of each kind described under [Ways of matching](methodological_background.qmd#ways-of-matching), and has not chosen between them:

- **Option A, direct covariate matching:** exact matching on diabetes, cirrhosis and HIV, limits on the difference in age and in calendar date, and preference for the control closest in earlier cost (months −12 to −4). Covariates not matched on are checked for balance and, if imbalanced, adjusted for in the outcome model.
- **Option B, time-varying propensity score matching:** a model estimates each person's probability of being diagnosed in each month from all the covariates in the plan, and each case is matched to the controls with the closest score at the same time since transplant, within 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the score.

The covariates cover demographics, transplant history, graft function, immunosuppression intensity, comorbidity, other infections, earlier use of care, earlier cost, dual Medicaid eligibility and region, all measured before time zero.

**Specified in:** [§6.1 Common elements](analysis-plan.qmd#61-common-elements), [§6.2 Covariates](analysis-plan.qmd#62-covariates), [§6.3 Option A](analysis-plan.qmd#63-option-a-direct-covariate-matching), [§6.4 Option B](analysis-plan.qmd#64-option-b-time-varying-propensity-score-matching).

**Current code:** the code implements a form of Option A. Controls are matched exactly on cirrhosis, HIV and diabetes, within 10 years of age and with time zero within 3 years of the case's, and a control with the same number of transplants is preferred. Each control is used once. It differs from the plan in these ways:

- cases are matched in order of how few potential controls they have, not in random order;
- among eligible controls, the first in row order is chosen, so the recorded seed has no effect ([#3](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/3));
- graft status and earlier cost are not matched on ([#8](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/8));
- the count of potential controls used to order cases checks Medicare coverage on a different date from the matching itself ([#6](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/6));
- cases for whom no control is found remain in the matched data ([#12](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/12)).

**Decision pending:** the lookback window for rejection treatment and the comorbidity index ([D5](analysis-plan.qmd#13-open-decisions)); the matching method ([D10](analysis-plan.qmd#13-open-decisions)); and, under Option B, whether to keep the limits on age and calendar date as well ([D6](analysis-plan.qmd#13-open-decisions)).

### Checking the match

A flow diagram follows the cohort from the source population through eligibility, case identification and matching, and baseline characteristics are reported by group before and after matching. Balance between cases and controls is judged with standardized mean differences at time zero, for every covariate in the plan and for cost in each of months −3, −2 and −1. An absolute standardized mean difference below 0.1 is taken as adequate. The number of cases with 2, 1 and 0 controls is reported, and unmatched cases are compared with matched ones.

**Specified in:** [§6.5 Matching outcomes reported](analysis-plan.qmd#65-matching-outcomes-reported), [§8.1 Descriptive analysis](analysis-plan.qmd#81-descriptive-analysis), [§8.2 Balance](analysis-plan.qmd#82-balance).

## Turning claims into costs

### Payments, not charges

Cost is measured as the amount **Medicare paid**; charges are not used ([Payments, not charges](methodological_background.qmd#payments-not-charges)). Payments made by patients, other insurers and Medicare Part D are not included.

Two payment fields are used: the Medicare payment on each institutional claim, and the Medicare payment on each physician/supplier line. Outpatient, home health and hospice claims also list payments for each revenue center (each department or type of service on the bill). These describe the same services as the claim's payment, so the two are never added together.

**Specified in:** [§7.1 Cost measure](analysis-plan.qmd#71-cost-measure); exclusions in [§11 Limitations](analysis-plan.qmd#11-limitations-to-address-in-reporting). The payment fields and the rule against adding revenue-center payments to claim payments come from the repository's [AGENTS.md](agents.qmd).

**Current code:** a separate 365-day summary also totals revenue-center payments, but separately from the claim totals, not added to them. The monthly costs used in the models come from institutional claim payments and physician/supplier line payments only.

### Spreading a stay across days

An institutional claim that covers several days is spread evenly across them ([Spreading a stay across days](methodological_background.qmd#spreading-a-stay-across-days) has a worked example).

**Specified in:** [§7.1 Cost measure](analysis-plan.qmd#71-cost-measure).

**Current code:** institutional claims are spread across days in the monthly costs. Physician/supplier lines are assigned to their first day of service. In the separate 365-day summary, institutional costs are spread across days but then selected by the claim's first day ([#11](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/11)).

### Adjusting for inflation

All costs are expressed in January 2021 US dollars.

**Specified in:** [§7.2 Inflation adjustment](analysis-plan.qmd#72-inflation-adjustment).

**Current code:** costs are adjusted with the medical care component of the Consumer Price Index, according to the month in which each claim began.

**Decision pending:** the price index ([D7](analysis-plan.qmd#13-open-decisions)).

### Months, partial months, and missing versus zero

Each person's follow-up is divided into 30-day months counted from time zero: month 0 is days 0 to 29, month −3 is days −90 to −61, and month 11, the last, is days 330 to 359. A person-month's cost is the sum of the spread, inflation-adjusted payments for that month. Covered months with no claims count as zero, uncovered months are left out, and partly observed months are kept with their days observed ([Missing versus zero cost](methodological_background.qmd#missing-versus-zero-cost)).

**Specified in:** [§7.3 Person-month structure](analysis-plan.qmd#73-person-month-structure).

**Current code:** a monthly total below zero, which can arise from claim adjustments, is set to zero. The plan does not yet describe this step. The separate 365-day summary covers days 0 to 365 (366 days), while the monthly costs cover days 0 to 359 ([#13](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/13)).

### End of follow-up

Follow-up ends at the earliest of:

- day 365 after time zero;
- death;
- loss of fee-for-service Medicare as primary payer;
- 1 January 2022.

Graft failure after time zero does not end follow-up. It is one of the ways cryptococcosis may lead to higher cost.

How death is handled defines what is being estimated ([Death and what is estimated](methodological_background.qmd#death-and-what-is-estimated)). If death ends follow-up, the estimate describes the excess cost of care while people are alive. If the months after death count as zero cost, the estimate also reflects the cost consequences of mortality. Whichever is chosen as primary, the other is a sensitivity analysis.

**Specified in:** [§7.4 End of follow-up](analysis-plan.qmd#74-end-of-follow-up).

**Current code:** follow-up ends at day 365, at 1 January 2022, or at the end of the period of Medicare coverage containing time zero. Death is not yet an end point ([#10](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/10)).

**Decision pending:** the treatment of death ([D8](analysis-plan.qmd#13-open-decisions)).

## Statistical analysis

### The primary model

Why the model describes mean cost directly is explained under [Why cost data are hard to model](methodological_background.qmd#why-cost-data-are-hard-to-model). Each person-month's cost is modelled with a generalized linear model that has:

- a **log link** and a **Tweedie distribution**;
- terms for group (case or control), month (as a category) and their interaction, so that the difference between cases and controls can differ in every month;
- the logarithm of the number of days observed as an **offset**.

What each of these does is explained under [Models for costs](methodological_background.qmd#models-for-costs). Standard errors come from generalized estimating equations clustered on matched set, and also on patient if one person can appear in more than one matched set.

**Specified in:** [§8.4 Primary model](analysis-plan.qmd#84-primary-model).

### From the model to a dollar estimate

1. For each month, the model predicts the mean cost of a full 30-day month for cases and for controls.
2. The case-minus-control difference is taken for each month.
3. The differences for months 0 to 11 are added up. That total is the excess cost per case.
4. A 95% confidence interval comes from a cluster bootstrap: matched sets are resampled with replacement 1,000 times (or more if needed), and the whole calculation is repeated each time.

The same model gives the month-by-month profile of excess cost from month −3 to month 11, and fitting it separately to each care setting (inpatient, outpatient, skilled nursing, home health, hospice, dialysis, physician/supplier) divides the excess by setting.

**Specified in:** [§8.4 Primary model](analysis-plan.qmd#84-primary-model), [§8.5 Secondary analyses](analysis-plan.qmd#85-secondary-analyses).

**Decision pending:** whether the estimate relies on matching alone, with months −3 to −1 reported as a balance check, or subtracts the difference between cases and controls before diagnosis as well, which gives a difference-in-differences estimate ([D9](analysis-plan.qmd#13-open-decisions)).

### What the code does now

The code fits five models to each of ten monthly cost outcomes: total cost, institutional cost, physician/supplier cost, and seven institutional categories (inpatient, outpatient, skilled nursing, home health, hospice, dialysis, and a non-claim/auxiliary category). None is yet the primary model as specified, and the total currently reported is not yet the planned estimate.

| Model | Outcome scale | Correlation handled by | Role in the plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tweedie, log link, mixed model | Dollars | Random intercept per patient | Closest to the primary model, but the plan uses generalized estimating equations clustered on matched set |
| Gaussian, identity link, mixed model | Dollars | Random intercept per patient | Sensitivity analysis |
| Gaussian, identity link, generalized estimating equations | Dollars | Exchangeable correlation within patient | Sensitivity analysis; currently used for the reported total |
| Gaussian on log(cost + 1), mixed model | Log dollars | Random intercept per patient | Sensitivity analysis |
| Gaussian on log(cost + 1), generalized estimating equations | Log dollars | Exchangeable correlation within patient | Sensitivity analysis |

Known differences from the plan:

- the reported total adds up each month's case–control difference measured relative to month −3, including months −2 and −1, so it is not the sum of differences over months 0 to 11 ([#2](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/2));
- the models on the dollar and log(cost + 1) scales include the days-observed offset in a way that only makes sense with a log link ([#4](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/4));
- the calculation for log-link models adds ratios rather than dollar differences ([#5](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/5));
- plots label back-transformed predictions from the log(cost + 1) and mixed models as mean dollars ([#14](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/14));
- the identifier of each matched set is not carried into the modelling data, so models can cluster only on patient (see also [#12](https://github.com/VagishHemmige/Cryptococcus-cost-analysis/issues/12));
- the cluster bootstrap is not yet implemented.

### Missing covariate data

Cost is observed only during covered months, by design. For covariates from the registry, missingness is described, and if it exceeds 5% for any covariate in a propensity score, multiple imputation by chained equations is used.

**Specified in:** [§8.6 Missing data](analysis-plan.qmd#86-missing-data).

## Sensitivity analyses

The planned sensitivity analyses test whether the result depends on particular choices:

- a sequential target-trial emulation, which compares people newly diagnosed in each month with everyone else eligible that month. The comparison group is weighted by the odds of diagnosis from a propensity score rather than matched, with weights truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles and untruncated results also reported;
- the other way of handling death ([D8](analysis-plan.qmd#13-open-decisions));
- outcome models on the dollar scale and on log cost;
- a stricter claims definition of cryptococcosis;
- one control per case, exact matching on cytomegalovirus infection, a narrower propensity score caliper under Option B, and the matching method not chosen as primary;
- recipients of a first transplant only;
- diagnoses before and after the October 2015 change in diagnosis codes.

**Specified in:** [§9 Sensitivity analyses](analysis-plan.qmd#9-sensitivity-analyses), [Appendix A](analysis-plan.qmd#appendix-a-target-trial-protocol).

## Reporting and limitations

The study will be reported following STROBE, its RECORD extension for routinely collected health data, and, for the target-trial analysis, TARGET. The limitations to be addressed include misclassification of diagnoses in claims, the restriction to Medicare payments and to fee-for-service Medicare, confounding by immunosuppression intensity that claims cannot fully measure, and coverage rules that change over time.

**Specified in:** [§11 Limitations](analysis-plan.qmd#11-limitations-to-address-in-reporting), [§12 Reporting map](analysis-plan.qmd#12-reporting-map).
